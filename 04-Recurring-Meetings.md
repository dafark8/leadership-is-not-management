# Chapter 4 Recurring Meetings

Inaugural, or kickoff, meetings are great for meeting your team. That is just the beginning. Meeting hygiene is a muscle. And while there is a push to no-meeting days or meeting-free cultures, meetings are key for building rapport. 

So how frequent, and with whom, should you be meeting? 

For newer teams, or more junior members, I suggest weekly 1:1s. As your team matures, or grows in size, you can likely drop to bi-weekly meetings. As you expand beyond your immediate sphere of influence, meetings may be monthly or quarterly. 

It is important not to have too many meetings. Personally I build many asynchronous rituals and tracking documents to still have visibility to our shared work, but not create unneeded meetings. 

## Regular Team 1:1s

**These are your report’s meetings. I cannot state this enough. These meetings are owned, managed, and for your report.** In an ideal world they own the invite but I understand the motivation for managers to coordinate their calendars. Share the controls. Let individuals edit the time if your calendar permissions allow for it. 
*Note: If you are in a leader position and not a people leader, these may feel awkward at first. Maintain communication with the team member’s reporting manager to coordinate feedback and support at an appropriate cadence.* 

The typical agenda for my team’s 30-minute 1:1s follows: 
- (5) General check in and catching up (not work related) 
- (15) Check in on the project. Directed by the team member, what do they want to share, present, or ask feedback on
- (5-10) minutes Leadership updates I need to share 

**When do you schedule 1:1s?** There’s a lot of debate on scheduling 1:1s at the start or end of the week. I like to focus on the back half (but not Friday). When thinking of a full week, I propose the following meeting cadence. Note all times are based on my local time: 

* 1:1s are reserved for Wednesday (2-5PM) or Thursday (10AM-3PM). 
* Try to have 30-minutes between 1:1s to allow for notes, tracking to dos, etc. This means in that 8-hour block there can be 8-10 1:1s a week. Hopefully you don’t have more direct reports than that. 
* Fridays are to focus on action items, map what was accomplished by the team in the previous week, and illustrate how I can support them in the next week. 
* Mondays are to initiate actions based on Friday's plan (if not already initiated). 
* Tuesdays and Wednesdays are administrative / organizational, following up on the various action items and other meetings as indicated below. 

This way, your reports aren’t burdened with 1:1s on a Friday when we only want to get to the weekend. Teams have Friday to reflect and act on changes before the weekend and it allows the weekend to be a clean break from work and for folks to actually unplug. 

I find 1:1s on Mondays often cause people to start the week on the wrong foot. If we discuss negative critiques from the week before, it may feel festered or lack immediate context. Be relevant. Be timely. And be respectful. 

**When to Email, when to Meet?** One lesson I learned over time is how to streamline my 1:1s. If there are updates I want to share across the team broadly, I’ve introduced a shared word document that I update weekly on Mondays. I follow up these updates with a team message with the link and highlights to be mindful of. 

The document includes team, department, and organizational updates and action items. It reserves a selection for “your questions”. This allows the team to review, in their own time, and raise relevant topics in 1:1s. Larger questions may also be addressed as a team. As an added bonus, this acts as a historical record of team updates and links when folks take PTO or missed an announcement. 

## Team, Skip Level, and Stakeholder 1:1s

If your team members Lead people of their own, get to know those contributors. This may go for ICs in product lead roles or if you are a manager-of-managers. 

Remember, everyone has a boss. Including your boss. Check in with them. Ideally these meetings are monthly but for the more senior level these may be bi-monthly or quarterly. Don’t let more than a full business quarter (3-months) go without checking in up or down the ladder. 

For your reports’ reports (down ladder) you can treat these similar to your regular 1:1s above. 

For meeting up ladder, with those more senior than you, have an agenda to highlight your achievements. Raise awareness of what you and your team has accomplished. Hopefully your manager champions for you and your team but this is your chance to frame the narrative. 

**On Walking Wins** One artifact I've iterated on in different roles is a *Walking Wins Deck*. The format has evolved based on organizational needs, but the intent is the same: Collect our recent projects in a Probelm > Solutions > Next Step (What/So What/Now What) slide format, following the larger organization's storytelling model. Link to the larger reports. This is a deck that can be shared asynchronously and referenced, and ideally pulled, into larger executive level conversations in a self-service model that give you and your team reach beyond your schedule. 

## Peer 1:1s

Who are your Leader peers? Who do you work with at a Leadership level in other departments? You should meet with these colleagues regularly. At least monthly. 

*“I just want to use this time to get to know you.”*

These meetings should be a mix of discussing project, individual, and organizational growth opportunities as well as personal conversations and simply connecting. A peer manager once called me out when I came to our 1:1s with an agenda on project-related items and they said *“David, I want to talk about this with you but let’s schedule another time so I can prepare. I just want to use this time to get to know you.”* 

This was genuine and heartwarming. And it made space for actually getting the real work done. 

## Your 1:1

You are someone’s report. You have a boss. Just as your reports should own their 1:1s, hopefully you have agency with your boss to lead those conversations. Ideally you meet weekly or biweekly. Here’s what to bring to your manager’s 1:1s: 

* General check in (remember, that humanity goes both ways) 
* What are some accomplishments / wins since last week? 
  * For you? 
  * For the team? 
* What are any blockers or obstacles you need your Leader’s help with? (I sometimes reframe this as Collaboration Opportunities)
  * For you? 
  * For the team? 
* How can I (the report) help you (my Leader) with larger organizational influences? 

## Random 1:1s

In my early 1:1s with new teams or organizations, I like to close our conversation by asking “who else should i know in the organization” and I schedule ad-hoc introductory calls with these people. These may be peers, or folks in entirely different departments. 

The point is to build bridges and relationships. You don’t want your first encounter with someone in marketing or legal to be a request for help. How can you humanize yourself to them, and them to you? Doing so is the crux of building relationships and stakeholder management. Be the person people think of when connections need to be made. That starts by building familiarity. 

Not all “random 1:1s” will lead to recurring meetings, In fact most won’t. That’s a good thing. Nobody has time for that. But if 10% of your random introductions build a working relationship, that is 10% greater reach and visibility you and your team have across the organization. 

## Relationships are Continuous NEW

Relationship are not one and done, not set and forget. 

It is a habit. Look to have one new 1:1 each month, and look look for one new recurring each quarter. Build relationship and network. Over time, you may find recurring meetings have run their course. It's OK to end series based on evolving business and personal needs. 

## Team Meetings

In addition to 1:1s, you should hold regular team meetings. These may be critiques, shares, retrospectives, or social times. My cadence and style of these have changed based on the needs of my team and there is no one right way. Various team meetings I have held include: 

**Monday Morning Coffee.** 30-minutes where we just talk about our weekends, and anything not work related. In meeting/work-heavy worlds, this keeps us honest on a human level. Ultimately my team took ownership of this call without me, allowing them to build rapport in a safe manager-free space. 

**Weekly critiques.** Allowing one team member to hone their presentation skills (and the rest of the team to hone their critique-offering muscle) in a safe environment. My general guidelines for critique are heavily inspired by Discussing Design by Adam Connor and Aaron Irizarry. My snapshot summary is pictured below. 

**Team process/general share.** A half-hour weekly sync for me to share what’s happening in the team and organization broadly. This offers a sounding board for team members in voicing concerns that can then be filtered to more senior leadership. I quickly dropped this meeting as a weekly ritual in lieu of the asynchronous document noted above. I still hold monthly or quarterly touch-points. 

**Scoping and Backlog** A weekly meeting with our Operations lead and senior leader reviewing our team's pipeline and priorities, allowing opportunity for cross-org pollination with the support of senior leadership. 

**One warning.** It is easy to over schedule your team. 1:1s. Non-work syncs. Critiques. Check Ins. Listen to your team. Look at the calendar. **Less is always more.** 

I have fallen into the trap of over scheduling and time and time again my team, and my peers, knock some sense into me. 

## A Note on Remote

Remote is here to stay

Your camera should be standard for 1:1 meetings and for group-activity meetings. 

But camera fatigue is a thing. If someone isn’t actively participating, it’s OK to have their camera off. If you just feel like crap, it’s OK to have a camera off. Having the camera off should be the exception, not the rule. As with everything, lead with kindness and trust your team. 

Mute yourself. If you’re not talking, go on mute. Even more, invest $40 in a directional mic to really boost your audio quality. There is nothing worse than hearing the keyboard clack of a colleague who is listening, but not participating in a dialogue.
