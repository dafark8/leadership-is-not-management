# Chapter 8 - Growing Your Team Members

Let’s assume you’ve done everything illustrated in my musings above. Or you’ve adapted it. Or ignored it entirely. Let’s assume you have a stellar team, and they all want to grow. How do individuals grow in their careers? 

*“My role/job is to help you find your next job”* 

I attribute the above to [Brian McElaney](https://www.linkedin.com/in/brianmcelaney/), and this is a perspective I have taken on myself. I believe there are three common paths to a promotion: 

1. A financial investment on part of the organization. The business decides to grow the team, which demands more Leaders. However leaders are often hired externally and investment like this is less common, especially in today’s market. 
2. Backfill a role. When someone in a Leadership position is promoted, or leaves the company, the business can choose to backfill internally by promoting from within. More often I see backfill roles filled externally or left vacant and existing team members inherit more responsibility. 
3. Job hopping. For better or worse, the hard truth is that the fastest way to a promotion is to look beyond the walls you currently work in. This may mean leaving the team and staying with the company, or may mean leaving the company altogether. 

I’ve been honest with my teams on these paths to a promotion. My goal is to find you your next job. Ideally within our team or within our organization. At the end of the day, I strive to see my team succeed. If they are unhappy or feel stagnated in growth opportunities, I want to help them find a home where they can thrive. After all, an unhappy team member is unlikely to bring their best ideas and craft. I want you to want to be part of the team. 

## What Does Your Team Want?

How do you keep folks engaged and excited? You need to focus on what the individual team member wants. What does recognition and achievement look like to them? What are their individual goals? And work with them individually. 

Some team members are motivated by titles. Is there a change in title or role that is achievable within the organization or team?

Some team members are motivated by compensation. Is there a budget for recognition? 

Some team members are motivated by working outside of project work. Is there opportunity for reach/stretch assignments to expand skill sets, while still honoring a typical work-week? 

## Set Expectations 

It is one thing to know where your team wants to go. It is another to see their potential. But unless you lead a team in a small organization, you likely are not the sole decider of promotions and elevations. 

Promotions are as much a business decision (is there scope, is there budget) as it is a professional one (can the person do it). 

In my experience, it is my role as a Leader to set expectations for the individual on the skills they need to acheive, and then my role to campaign for the budget and case across leadership. 

For my team member, this means some harder 1:1 conversations setting stretch goals and being explicit that these are the keys to unlocking the next level (without making promises) 

At the leadership level, this means working with my peer group to find opportunities for that team member to excel, coordinating feedback across design, product, and technology, and acting as a filter allowing my team members to focus on their growth areas while continuing to succeed at their "business as usual" assignments. 

## Show the Path

One aspect often missing in teams is a clear, documented, and shared career path. This is key regardless of the team members' motivations. Knowing where someone wants to go, and having a clear path forward are two different things. 

Career Ladders are key to team and individual success. I’ve worked on a handful of career ladders and there are two common threads I would stress in your exploration: 
1. Be clear in your expectations. Communicate individual gaps and growth opportunities. 
2. Have both individual practitioner and managerial paths, so folks can see themselves choosing the path best for them, not the path towards higher compensation. 

While the career frameworks I’ve developed are bespoke to specific teams and organizations, I appreciate [Peter Merholz’s Career Trellis Framework](https://www.petermerholz.com/blog/new-masterclass-from-ladder-to-trellis-flexible-career-architectures-for-ux-teams/). One question I pose is at what point do individuals choose between the IC-path and the Management-path. I strive for 3-4 levels per group before the branching. My high-level structure is illustrated below: 

| Generic Level | Common Title (IC Path) | Common Title (Managerial Path)|
| --- | --- | --- |
| IC Level 1 | Designer/Researcher | NA | 
| IC Level 2 | Senior | NA |
| IC Level 3 | Lead | NA |
| IC Level 4 | Principal | NA |
| Leader Level 1 | Practice Lead | Team Manager (1-3) |
| Leader Level 2 | Senior Practice Lead | Senior Manager (3-6)|
| Leader Level 3 | Senior Practice Lead 2 | Director (Manages Managers)| 
| Leader Level 4 | Principal Practice Lead | Senior Director (Manages Departments) |
